Ingredients & Spices — three ways to read a pantry

All three use China's real data. The shared move: drop the emoji grid, connect every ingredient to either the flavor fingerprint axes or its job in the dish, and let hierarchy do the work. Axis colors are consistent across all three.

Prototype A

The flavor matrix

A quiet table: one row per ingredient, one column per fingerprint axis. A solid dot = main contribution, a faint dot = supporting note. You can read it two ways — across a row ("what does doubanjiang do?") or down a column ("where does all that umami come from?"). Tier headers stay, but reduced to a label + one-line role. Staples get a sentence instead of rows, because a canvas has no flavor column.

Prototype B

Why it tastes this way

Inverts the hierarchy: lead with the cuisine's fingerprint axes, strongest first, each with its intensity bar — the same 1–10 the radar shows. Under each axis: the ingredients that put it there, with a small tier tag. This answers the question the pyramid never answers: "China scores 9 on umami — where does that come from?" It's the strongest tie-in to the flavor fingerprint, and it doubles as radar education.

Prototype C

The build — tiers as cooking order

Keeps your four tiers but makes them a sequence instead of a pyramid: read top to bottom and you're watching a dish get assembled. Each tier gets a plain-language job description; ingredients are quiet chips whose left edge is colored by their dominant axis (that's the fingerprint tie-in, kept subtle). This is the version that explains how the tiers work together.
